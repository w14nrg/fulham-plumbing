Why has my shower pressure dropped?

By Fulham PlumbingLast updated 2026-10-01

Short answer

Start by deciding whether the drop is sudden or gradual and whether it affects hot, cold or both. A blocked head is easy to rule out, but a failing pump, partly closed valve or wider supply problem needs proper diagnosis before parts are replaced.

Is it sudden or gradual?

A sudden change usually points to something that has moved, blocked, failed or been isolated. Check whether another tap in the property has changed at the same time, because that tells you whether the problem belongs to the shower or to the wider supply.

A gradual decline is more consistent with limescale, a cartridge slowly restricting, a pump losing performance or a valve becoming stiff. Make a note of when you first noticed it and whether the problem is worse at busy times or only after the system has been unused.

Check the shower head and hose

Unscrew the shower head if the design allows and run the hose briefly into the tray or bath. If the flow becomes much stronger, the head is probably scaled or restricted. A hose can also collapse internally even when the outside looks normal.

Soaking a removable head in a suitable descaling solution can restore small spray holes. Rinse it thoroughly afterwards. If the restriction remains with the head removed, do not keep buying accessories; the cause is further back in the valve, supply or pump.

Is it only hot, only cold, or both?

Compare the shower with nearby taps. Weak hot but normal cold points towards the hot-water side, while both sides being weak suggests a common restriction or a shower-valve issue. If only the shower is affected, the fault is more likely local to the shower or its pump.

Where hot water is tank-fed and cold is direct from the mains, the two sides can behave very differently. A thermostatic valve needs the correct operating range for that arrangement, otherwise temperature and flow can both become unstable.

Valves that are part-closed

Isolation valves under floors, in airing cupboards or behind panels can be left partly closed after other work. Some older service valves also fail internally and restrict flow even though the handle looks open.

Do not force a valve that feels seized. If a stopcock or service valve is visibly corroded, forcing it can turn a pressure problem into a leak. A plumber can test flow before and after the valve and replace it if the restriction is proven.

Pump problems in tank-fed systems

A shower pump may fail to start, run noisily or deliver less water than it used to. Airlocks, blocked inlet filters, worn bearings and a failed flow switch are all possible. A pump should not be replaced until the tank supply and valves have been checked.

A shower fitted close to the level of the storage tank may also need a negative-head pump. If the wrong pump type was installed, it can be reluctant to start even when the motor itself is healthy.

Low pressure across the whole house

If several outlets are weak, check that the internal stopcock is fully open and compare upstairs with downstairs. Old narrow supply pipes, shared supplies and restrictions at the main valve can all reduce usable flow.

A sudden whole-property drop can also come from a supplier-side issue. Check current Thames Water service information, then arrange pressure and flow testing if the problem remains. A pump is not automatically the answer to poor incoming supply.

When to call a plumber

Call when the simple head-and-hose checks do not isolate the problem, when a pump is involved, or when valves feel seized. Proper testing should tell you whether the next step is a cartridge, a valve repair, pump work or a wider supply solution.

Avoid opening pump electrics or removing concealed shower valves without knowing how they are isolated. Water close to electrical equipment is a point to stop DIY rather than experiment.

What changed just before the pressure dropped?

Think back to any work carried out immediately before the change. A recently serviced tap, an isolated cylinder, work in the loft or a valve that was turned off and reopened can leave an isolation valve partly closed or introduce air into a stored-water system. That history can save a lot of unnecessary dismantling.

If nothing in the property changed, compare the shower at different times and compare it with a cold kitchen tap. Consistent weakness at every outlet points in a different direction from a shower that fades only on hot water or only after the pump starts. Write down those differences before the visit; they are more useful than simply saying the pressure feels low.

  • Note whether the change was sudden or gradual.
  • Check if hot, cold or both are affected.
  • Compare one nearby tap with the shower.
  • Tell the plumber about recent isolation or plumbing work.

When to call a plumber

Call when the restriction is beyond the removable head or hose, when pressure is poor at several outlets, or when a pump or seized valve is involved.

Questions

Can limescale really cause it?

Yes. Scale can block a shower head, strainers and cartridges, especially with hard London water. The useful check is whether flow improves when the removable head is taken out of the equation.

Why only the hot side?

The hot supply may be tank-fed, restricted by a valve or affected by the cylinder arrangement while the cold remains on mains pressure.

Will a new shower head fix it?

Only if the old head is the restriction. If the hose flow is already weak, a new head will not correct the underlying supply problem.
