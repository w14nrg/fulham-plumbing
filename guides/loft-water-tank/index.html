Do I still need a cold water tank in the loft?

By Fulham PlumbingLast updated 2026-10-01

Short answer

A loft tank may still be doing an important job: supplying a vented hot-water cylinder, toilets or tank-fed showers. Do not remove it until you know exactly what it feeds and whether the property can work safely without stored water.

What a loft tank actually does

A cold-water storage tank holds water above the outlets it supplies. Gravity then creates head for a vented cylinder, some cold outlets or a shower pump. The tank also provides a reserve of stored water for parts of the system that are not connected directly to the incoming main.

Properties are often altered over many years, so the original arrangement may no longer match what is connected today. A tank that appears unused from the hatch can still feed one toilet, a cylinder or a shower. Tracing the outlets matters before any pipe is capped.

How to tell what it feeds

Look for labelled valves, visible outlet pipes and the position of the hot-water cylinder. A plumber can isolate or lower the tank safely and observe which outlets stop, provided that testing does not risk damaging pumps or other equipment.

Do not simply close a valve and leave a long branch of old pipe full of stagnant water. Redundant branches should be removed or altered properly once their purpose is understood. That is especially important when a system has been partly converted to mains pressure.

Signs the tank needs attention

A dripping overflow, constant filling, rust, sediment, a missing lid, damaged insulation or damp around the base all deserve investigation. A steel tank showing corrosion should not be trusted merely because it has not leaked yet.

Noise is another clue. A worn float valve can chatter or bang as it closes, and a restricted inlet may take a very long time to refill after heavy use. Those faults can often be repaired without replacing the tank itself.

Why the overflow is dripping

The overflow runs when the water level rises above its intended point. The usual cause is a float valve that does not shut fully because of wear, scale, poor adjustment or a damaged float.

Replacing or servicing the valve can be a small repair. The tank should still be inspected at the same time because a very old shell, poor support or missing lid may make a wider replacement more sensible.

Keep, replace or remove?

Keep a sound tank when the system depends on stored water and the tank is properly covered, supported and maintained. Replacement makes sense when the shell, access, fittings or hygiene condition are poor but the stored-water arrangement still suits the home.

Removal is a system change rather than a tidy-up job. Every outlet it feeds needs another suitable water source, and the incoming main must provide adequate flow. A weak or shared supply can make a direct conversion unattractive.

What removal involves

The plumber traces each outlet, confirms the hot-water arrangement and plans how anything that remains will be supplied. The old tank is drained, disconnected and removed where access allows, and redundant pipe is dealt with rather than left as a hidden stagnant branch.

Some homes need more design work before stored water can be removed. A shower pump, vented cylinder or upper-floor outlet may depend on that tank in a way that is not obvious from the room below.

Lids, insulation and hygiene

A modern storage tank should have a close-fitting lid and protected openings for the vent and overflow. The aim is to keep dust, insects and debris out while allowing the system to vent safely.

Insulation should protect the tank and associated pipes from freezing. The exact arrangement in a loft matters because heat rising from the rooms below can help protect the tank, while exposed pipes at the eaves can freeze first.

Galvanised and very old tanks

Old galvanised steel tanks can corrode internally and collect sediment. Their shape and size can also make removal through a small hatch difficult, even after the plumbing has been disconnected.

Modern plastic tanks are available in different shapes, and sectional tanks can help with very restricted access. Safe boarded access and support remain essential; the best tank in the wrong position is still a poor installation.

Pumps and stored water

A shower pump normally relies on stored water rather than drawing directly from the incoming main. Removing a tank without redesigning that supply can leave the pump starved, noisy or unable to operate.

If a shower is close to the water level in the tank, the pump type also matters. The tank decision and pump decision should therefore be made together rather than as two unrelated jobs.

Before deciding on a change

Write down which outlets are weak, whether the home has a vented cylinder, whether any pump is fitted and whether the incoming mains flow is known to be good. Those facts make a site visit much more useful.

A plumber can then explain the realistic choices: service the existing tank, replace it, improve associated valves and pipework, or redesign the system if the property genuinely no longer needs stored water.

When to call a plumber

Call when the overflow runs, the tank is corroded, safe access is poor, or you are considering removal and do not know what the tank still supplies.

Questions

Can I get rid of my loft tank?

Possibly, but only after tracing everything it supplies and confirming the incoming mains can support the new arrangement.

Why is my overflow dripping?

A fill valve or float usually is not shutting at the intended level, although the tank and overflow connection should also be inspected.

How old is too old for a tank?

Condition matters more than one age. Corrosion, missing lids, poor supports and repeated valve faults are stronger reasons to act.

Will a replacement fit through the hatch?

That has to be measured before ordering. Different tank shapes and sectional designs can solve some restricted-access lofts.
